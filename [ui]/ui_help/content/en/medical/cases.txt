# Example patients
@keywords: example, examples, case, cases, patient, scenario, walkthrough, gunshot, shock, cardiac arrest, VF, overdose, opioid, hypoglycaemia, diabetes, ketoacidosis, house fire, burns, intubation, stroke, asthma
@related: medical, medical_diagnosis, medical_monitor, medical_drugs, medical_procedures

Real patients do not come with a label. These examples show how to put the signs together and in what order to treat. The numbers are typical; every patient is a little different.

## 1. Gunshot to the torso – haemorrhagic shock

**Call:** a man was shot in a street fight. He lies on the pavement.

**What you find:** dazed. Injury: **Gunshot wound – Critical, Untreated**. Bleeding: **Critical**. Skin: **pale, cold, clammy**. Breathing rapid. Feeling the pulse: 135.

**Thinking:** critical bleeding loses 12 ml every second – in about 3 minutes the heart stops. The pale, clammy skin and the racing pulse say he already lost a lot. Everything else waits.

1. **Bandage** at once (with two medics: one bandages, the other takes the bag and places the IV).
2. **IV access** – the veins are collapsing, it is harder than usual. Fluids start running.
3. Attach the **monitor**: pressure 70/45, SpO2 91%. **O2 mask**.
4. Watch the pressure climb as the fluids run. Do not give Fentanyl yet: it lowers the pressure.
5. When the systolic is over 90: stretcher, ambulance, hospital. The critical wound still oozes a little – do not wait on scene.

**If the heart stops first:** the monitor most likely shows **PEA**. CPR, but the heart cannot restart while half of the blood is missing: the IV fluids are the real treatment. Then Adrenalin.

## 2. Collapsed on the sidewalk – VF arrest

**Call:** a 55 year old man collapsed in front of a shop. A passer-by says he grabbed his chest.

**What you find:** **Clinical death**, Time left 04:40, **no pulse**, not breathing, skin cyanotic.

1. **CPR** immediately – every good round buys 45 seconds.
2. Your partner attaches the **monitor**: **Ventricular Fibrillation**. Shockable!
3. When the CPR round ends (or is interrupted): **CHARGE**, wait for the tone, **SHOCK**.
4. **Shock delivered – continue CPR, check the rhythm.** Next CPR round. Meanwhile the partner places the **IV access** and gives **Adrenalin**.
5. The rhythm changes during CPR: **Pulseless Ventricular Tachycardia** – shock again.
6. **Pulse restored (ROSC).** Pulse 118, pressure 78/50, SpO2 60%.
7. After ROSC: O2 mask, fluids are running, measure the **glucose**. He is unconscious and snoring: **RSI** (Ketamine, wait, Rocuronium, wait 15 s, Intubate).
8. The monitor suddenly shows **Ventricular Tachycardia with Pulse**, pressure falling: **SYNC**, CHARGE, SHOCK – cardioversion. Without SYNC the shock can send it back to VF.
9. Stable rhythm, systolic over 90: transport.

>! If the monitor shows **Asystole** or **PEA**: no shock. CPR + Adrenalin, and look for the cause.

## 3. Unconscious in the park – which poison?

**Call:** a young man unresponsive on a park bench.

**What you find:** unconscious, **no visible injuries**. Breathing **slow, shallow**. Skin cyanotic. Pulse 55.

**Thinking:** unconscious without an injury: sugar, poison, stroke, seizure, head injury? Rule them out one by one.

1. **O2 mask** – the SpO2 is low, he barely breathes.
2. **Glucometer**: 92 mg/dL – normal, not the sugar.
3. **Neuro exam**: **Responds to pain only**, **Pupils: pinpoint**, **Needle marks on the arms**, **FAST: not assessable**.
4. Pinpoint pupils + slow breathing + needle marks = **opioid overdose**. **Naloxone** (nose spray, no IV needed).
5. Within a short time he wakes up, the breathing becomes normal, the SpO2 rises.

**If the pupils were "normal size, sluggish" with empty pill packets nearby:** sleeping pills. Naloxone would do nothing: keep him on oxygen, intubate if he starts snoring, and take him to hospital.
**If they were dilated, with a very fast pulse and sweating:** stimulants – Midazolam.

## 4. The confused diabetic – too low and too high

**4a.** A woman behaves strangely at a bus stop. **Confused**, skin **pale, sweaty**, pulse 100.

1. Pale + sweaty + confused: measure the **glucose**: **52 mg/dL**. Hypoglycaemia.
2. Confused but can she swallow? Yes (confused counts as awake): **Oral glucose gel** (+40). If she were dazed or unconscious: IV access and **Glucose 40%**.
3. Measure again after a minute or two: it should be over 70 and she becomes oriented. If it falls again, give more.

**4b.** A man found at home, **confused**, breathing **deep and rapid**, skin **pale**, low pressure, fast pulse.

1. **Glucometer**: **520 mg/dL**. The **neuro exam** finds a **fruity (acetone) smell** – diabetic ketoacidosis.
2. **IV access** – he lost a lot of fluid with the sugar (that is why he is pale), the fluids raise his pressure and slowly lower the sugar.
3. **Insulin** – once, after measuring. It works slowly over 15 minutes.
4. Measure every few minutes. Never give a second Insulin "to be sure": an overshoot is a hypoglycaemia.

## 5. House fire – burns and a burned airway

**Call:** a man carried out of a burning house. Soot around the mouth.

**What you find:** dazed. Injuries: **Burn – Critical**, **Suffocation – Minor**. Pain 9/10. Breathing laboured. Monitor: SpO2 84% and falling, pressure 110/70.

**Thinking:** a critical burn means the airway is burned too: it will swell, and the O2 mask cannot fix it. Intubate early, while the SpO2 still allows it – it is the hardest intubation (blood, swollen tissue).

1. **O2 mask** immediately (pre-oxygenation).
2. **IV access** – the burn leaks plasma, the pressure will fall.
3. **Ketamine** – he falls asleep, the pain is gone.
4. **Rocuronium** – 15 seconds, then **Intubate**. The SpO2 falls during the attempt: be quick. Use `E` to suction the blood.
5. **Bandage** the burn (dressing) – the plasma loss nearly stops.
6. Transport. Watch the **oxygen** in the bag: the tube needs it all the way to the handover.

## 6. Stroke – keep the pressure, move fast

**Call:** an old woman suddenly cannot speak properly.

**What you find:** **confused**, no visible injury. Monitor: pressure 165/100, sinus rhythm.

1. **Neuro exam**: **FAST: face droops on the left side**, **left arm drifts down, weak grip**, **speech slurred, words mixed up**. Stroke.
2. **Glucometer** – low sugar can look like a stroke. 105: normal.
3. Do NOT give Captopril: a systolic between 130 and 220 is what her brain needs. Too low or too high makes the stroke worse 2.5 times faster.
4. O2 mask if the SpO2 is low. The stroke worsens every 8 minutes: no time to waste, transport at once.
5. If she becomes unconscious and starts snoring: the airway is at risk – RSI.

## 7. Asthma attack

**Call:** a teenager cannot breathe after running.

**What you find:** **wheezing**, **speaks in short phrases only**, pulse 120, SpO2 85%.

1. **O2 mask** and **Salbutamol** (inhaled, no IV needed).
2. The SpO2 rises, the breathing calms down. Salbutamol raises the pulse a little – that is normal.
3. **Warning sign:** **Silent chest, exhausted** / **cannot speak** – almost no air moves. Salbutamol + O2 at once; if the SpO2 keeps falling under 75%, RSI.
